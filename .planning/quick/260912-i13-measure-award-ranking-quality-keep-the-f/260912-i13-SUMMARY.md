# Can we rank every team at an event by how likely they are to win each award?

**Yes for the order, no for the number, and those are both the answer.**

On the flagship judged awards the winner lands in a top-3 of a ~40-team pool
between 32.3% and 52.2% of the time against a random ~8%, with a median winner
rank of 3-6. That is a real capability and it is meaningfully more than the
top-1 number that 5n8 and 7bp reported: top-3 is 1.9x to 2.7x top-1 on every
one of them, so the ordering below rank 1 carries genuine information rather
than being the top pick plus noise.

But on three of those five types the best ordering is still **B1** — "sort by
who has won things before" — not the fitted model: Impact 51.1% vs 48.4%,
Engineering Inspiration 32.3% vs 28.7%, Safety 52.2% vs 42.9% on R@3. The fit
wins Excellence in Engineering (33.8% vs 26.1%) and Autonomous (41.4% vs
36.1%). Every one of those five gaps is outside the measured 1.50pp R@3 band,
so all five are real in both directions. This is the same result 5n8 reported
on top-1, and keeping the whole ordering did not change it.

And **the probabilities are not usable as stated.** All five flagship types
fail the pre-committed calibration rule, all five by overconfidence: Impact's
top pick is stated at 29.9% and wins 21.2%; Engineering Inspiration's at 17.0%
and wins 9.5%. Pooled across every judged type the no-age arm's reliability
buckets are off by 14.7pp at [0.35, 0.5) and 24.4pp at [0.5, 1). The Brier
skill score is positive (0.0338 pooled) — the probabilities do carry
information beyond "everyone is equally likely" — but the levels are wrong, and
a number on a page would be a lie.

There is a second, sharper way to say it: **on the three types where the
ordering is best, the thing producing that ordering states no probability at
all.** B1 is a sort, not a model. So the best available ranking and the only
available probability are two different objects, and the better one is the one
that cannot tell you how confident it is.

### T1 — the ordering that already existed

`pickByWeights` built a utility vector over every team in the pool and threw
all but the argmax away. Every ordering is now **derived** from the pick that
already existed rather than built beside it: `scoreByWeights` is the
dot-product loop lifted verbatim, `pickByWeights` is `argmaxIndex` of it, and
B1/B2/RB1/RB2 each get a full ordering from the comparator their pick already
implemented plus candidate index as a final key, so the order is total even for
two unparseable team keys.

That derivation is what makes **recall@1 reproduce the existing top-1 accuracy
mechanically rather than by assertion** — verified exact on all 156 pooled
predictor rows. It is the control for the whole extension.

Two denominators, applied to all seven predictors: recall@k and MRR keep every
instance and score an unreachable or abstained one 0; meanRank/medRank/norm%
exclude it and count it. B0 is the exact random null, closed-form, proved
against brute-force enumeration. The leak tests were extended over the new
state.

### T2 — the calibration

Softmax over each arm's own utility vector. Two exclusions, each counted and
printed: thin-prior rows emit no probability (a heuristic stood in, and
fabricating one would be the dishonest option), and multi-recipient instances
break the sum-to-1 premise. An unreachable instance is deliberately **not** a
third exclusion — a total mass of 1 on a pool containing no winner is real
overconfidence, not an artifact.

The verdict rule was committed before any number was seen: usable iff skill >
0 **and** the top-1 gap is within +/-5pp **and** no bucket with >= 50 slots is
off by more than 10pp. The script prints the verdict and the failing clauses
itself, per arm. Both arms come out **NEEDS RECALIBRATION**.

### T3 — the bands, and the practical answer

`NOISE_MARGIN_PP = 1` was measured for top-1 accuracy only, and there was no
reason it transferred to a recall cutoff or an MRR. Measuring it rather than
assuming it produced the finding that **it does not transfer**: R@3's real band
is 1.50pp, half again as wide as the inherited constant, so a 1.2pp R@3 gap
scored against 1.0pp would have been published as a result and been noise. The
normalized rank percentile is the most stable metric measured by a factor of
four (0.17pp), which makes it the most trustworthy for comparing predictors.

Five metrics have no measured band and print `CANNOT BE SCORED` rather than
borrowing one.

## The defect T3 caught

The per-type READING block initially compared `norm%` across the RB
denominator boundary. On type 14 Highest Rookie Seed it scored the model's
18.0% — of a ~40-team pool — against RB2's 64.3% — of a ~6-team rookie block —
and printed **BETTER** on a 46pp gap that was entirely the denominator.

That is 260912-7bp's structural-zero artifact in a third outfit, and it is
exactly the failure the plan's load-bearing requirement exists to prevent,
arriving through a door the plan had not thought to guard. `isMetricComparable`
now refuses to score `norm%`, `meanRank` or `medRank` against a rookie
baseline, driven by an explicit `perOrderingLength` flag on the band constant.
Recall@k and MRR are unaffected: both keep every scored instance as their
denominator, so an abstaining RB scores 0 and stays in, and the two predictors
really are measured over the same population.

## Pre-committed readings, as they came out

1. **"Ranking is useful only if the model's R@3 beats B1's by more than the
   band."** Met on 2 of 5 flagship types (Excellence in Engineering +7.6pp,
   Autonomous +5.3pp). Not met on 3 (Impact -2.7pp, Engineering Inspiration
   -3.5pp, Safety -9.3pp), where the honest statement is the same one 5n8 made:
   the decoration ordering is better than the fit.
2. **"Ranking is a genuinely different capability from top-1 only if R@3 and
   R@5 are far enough above R@1 to change what a user could do with it."** Met,
   clearly. Top-3 is 1.9x-2.7x top-1 and the winner's median rank is 3-6 of
   ~40. The plan committed to saying the ordering below the top pick was weak
   if the numbers had come out flat; they did not.
3. **"The rookie types are read against RB1/RB2, never B1/B2."** Held, and
   enforced in code by `rankReferencePredictor` and `ROOKIE_AWARD_TYPES` rather
   than by prose.
4. **"Types 1 and 2 stay reference-only."** Held, now with the multi-recipient
   recall inflation named in the section header: winner rank is the best of 3-4
   recipients, so recall@k has three or four chances to land inside k where a
   judged award has one.
