<content_spec>
## The full list of facts the page may state

Every measured number below comes from `260909-3fj-RESEARCH.md`. Nothing else may be stated as a measurement.

**Seven sections, in this order. Ids are pinned by equality in Task 1's test.**

### 1. `where-the-number-starts` (figure: `even-split`)
- FRC never records what a single robot scored. TBA publishes alliance totals only.
- So every per robot number on this page is worked out from alliance scores, not measured directly.
- One team's miss in one match is the alliance's actual score minus its predicted score, divided by the number of robots on the alliance. That team's even share of the miss.
- Worked example, used by figure F1: predicted 150, actual 168, a miss of 18 points, 6 points each.
- Splitting it evenly is a choice, and it is the honest one: nobody knows who did what, and any other split would be made up.

### 2. `one-team-many-matches` (figure: `deviations`)
- Collect one team's misses across its matches, oldest first.
- Recent matches count more. Each match further back counts about 0.89 as much as the one after it, so a match 6 back counts half as much as the newest one. The half life is 6 matches.
- Measure the spread of those misses about the team's own average miss, not about zero.
- Why that matters: a model that misses a team by the same amount every match has shown no swing at all. That steady gap is the model's problem, not the robot's. Measured on OPR before this centring, one team came out at ±298.92 against a rating of 322.42, nearly all of it a steady bias.
- Multiply by 1.92 so the answer reads in points.
- It needs at least two played matches. One match cannot tell a steady bias apart from a swing.
- Exactly 0 is a real answer, not a bug: a robot the model misses by the same amount every time is perfectly consistent.
- Worked out once when the site publishes its data, not in your browser.

### 3. `what-a-big-swing-means` (figure: `same-rating`)
- The `±` is not how sure the site is about the rating. It is how much a robot's contribution moves match to match.
- A robot with a small `±` plays about the same every match. A robot with a large `±` might dominate one match and barely show up in the next.
- Two robots can carry the exact same rating and a completely different `±`.
- Why a scout cares, all three stories: a top seed picking first usually wants a LOW `±`, a robot that turns up the same every match. A low seed hunting an upset wants a HIGH `±`, because it needs the variance to have any shot at all. Mid quals, it is how you judge whether a partner can be relied on.
- Where you see it today: a grey `±` beside the Total on a team page and in the Total column of the Teams list, to two decimals. The `±` toggle in the ribbon turns every `±` on the site off at once.
- It is worked out for every published algorithm, OPR, EPA and BPR, from nothing but the predicted score, the actual score and the roster. The same robot carries a different `±` under each one.

### 4. `three-robots-one-band` (figure: `squares-add`)
- An alliance is three robots. Their swings combine by adding the squares and taking the square root.
- Three robots at ±10 give ±17.32, not ±30, because they do not all swing the same way in the same match.
- All or nothing: if any robot on the alliance has no Swing Factor yet, that alliance gets no band at all. Better no band than a band that is too tight.

### 5. `reading-the-match-band` (figure: `match-band`)
- On a team page and on an event page, every match row draws both alliances on one shared score axis.
- The soft bar is one standard deviation either side of the predicted score. The solid tick is the predicted score. The ringed dot is what the alliance actually scored.
- The overlap between the two bands is the win probability, drawn rather than asserted. Heavy overlap is close to a coin flip. Clean separation is a strong favourite.
- The band on a match uses only the matches played before it, never anything from later in the season. It is not reset between events.
- A missing band is missing on purpose. See section 4.

### 6. `how-the-two-constants-were-picked`
- The half life of 6 was swept walk forward over 275,172 team matches from 2024 to 2026. It sits at the top of a plateau running roughly 4 to 12.
- Weighting recent matches beats a flat average by only 2.3%, and the code says so.
- The 1.92 scale was measured on 86,844 alliance observations, by checking how far alliances actually landed from their predictions against their three robots' own unscaled swing.
- The first attempt at that scale was circular. It compared a team's miss against that same team's past misses and duly recovered about 1.0. It is written down in the code so nobody repeats it.
- Both constants are permanently shut out of parameter tuning. The tuner scores predictions and is blind to a display number, so tuning could only ever chase a better looking `±`.

### 7. `what-it-cannot-do`
- The best correlation anyone can get for predicting a team's actual miss in its next match is about 0.59. That is the data's limit, not the formula's, because FRC records no per robot score.
- The band is deliberately wide. It covers about 76% to 87% of actual scores depending on which rating you are looking at, against 68.3% for a textbook one standard deviation band.
- Checked again on 2026, over 31,142 alliance observations, the scale that would make the band a textbook one standard deviation came out near 2.0.
- A separate scale per rating would fix the width, and it was deliberately not adopted: three constants would need re measuring on every model change, which is exactly the kind of stale number this project's failure log is about.
- An independent audit over 36,805 alliance observations across 216 events found the typical miss was 0.920 band widths, with 75.2% of results inside one band width, 96.3% inside two and 99.5% inside three.
- Alliances score a little above their prediction on average, by 0.1197 band widths, and the misses lean high, so you are about 21% more likely to be surprised high than low.
- It says nothing at all until a team has played two matches.
